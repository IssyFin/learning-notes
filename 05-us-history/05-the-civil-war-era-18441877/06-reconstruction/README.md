### Reconstruction and Response (~1860s)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2b144de2a6568048a27ce8cd52253441)*

During reconstruction, the South issued Black Codes to maintain the economic and social hierarchy. Congress responded by passing the 13th, 14th, and 15th amendments

#### Page notes

- Black Codes
  - As Civil War drew to a close, Southern States passed “Black Codes” which aimed to economically and politically disable Black Americans
    - Designed to maintain the social and economic hierarchy and codified white supremacy
    - Disallowed Black Americans from voting, serving on juries, own/carry weapons, and own/lease land
  - By apprenticeship laws, judges bound many Black orphans to white plantation owners, effectively forcing them into similar labor conditions they were subject to under slavery

![African American men working in postbellum Tennessee under unfair labor conditions. Image courtesy of World Digital Library.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/cd7995b486f4196a8a82e22eedb70a951686c940.png)

- The Rise of Radical Republicanism
  - Black Codes spurred the Joint Committee on Reconstruction into action; members thought thirteenth amendment didn’t go far enough, and Southern states weren’t punished enough for the Civil War.
  - Congress extended life of the Freedmen’s Bureau to combat Black Codes and passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866, establishing citizenship for Black Americans
    - Contradicted the 1857 Dred Scott decision
    - Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act, but was overridden by Congress
    - Civil Rights Act followed by the 14th and 15th amendments
- The First KKK
  - Ku Klux Klan founded as a “social club” and operated as a network of local terrorist groups
  - Committed acts of violence undercover; estimated to have committed over 3,500 racially motivated lynchings between 1865 and 1900
    - Also targeted white sympathizers, labeled “carpetbaggers” and “scalawags”
    - Often killed Republican candidates to secure Democratic victory
    - Frequently denied Black voters, either committing fraud to dilute the vote, or by barring Black Americans from voting through poll tests
  - Congress responded to the KKK and the terror it caused via the Force Acts of 1870.
    - Required South to recognize the guarantees of the 14th amendment
    - Issued the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 shortly thereafter to further combat the KKK; made it federally punishable to commit hate crimes or disenfranchise based on race
  - As KKK countermeasures closed, KKK resurged, their biggest waves in the 1920s (following rise of nativism) and 1950s (following Brown vs Board of Education). Still exists today.
- The Freedmen’s Bureau
  - Established in 1865 to help freed people achieve economic stability and political freedoms
    - Also known as the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands
    - Received charter for one year under Lincoln
    - Intended to operate as a primitive welfare agency to ease transition from slavery to freedom
      - Helped freed people gain labor contracts and delivered food to the poor
    - Supported education, establishing public schools for freed people and poor whites to receive elementary and higher education
      - Includes Fisk Hampton, and Dillard Universities
  - Many white southerners (including President Johnson) challenged the legitimacy of the bureau
    - Resented the new schools and welfare programs; denounced as a “waste of money” and a “foolish effort”
    - Johnson vetoed the renewal of the charter, but Congress overrode the veto
    - Due to pressures from white southerners, Congress dismantled the Bureau in 1872
- Failure of Reconstruction
  - Community and Change in the Reconstruction Era
    - Freedom: 13th amendment
      - Pre-13th amendment: Black people didn’t have physical or economic freedom; no choice of work or money
      - Post 13th amendment: Still victims of:
        - Land redistribution was overturned by Johnson
        - Sharecropping (freedom over work but little economic freedom since cycle of debt and poverty)
        - Black Codes
        - Segregation
    - Citizenship: 14th amendment:
      - Pre-14th amendment: Citizenship limited to whites, per the Dred Scott decision
      - Post-14th amendment
        - Citizenship protections for anyone born on US soil
        - Supreme Court defined narrowly in the late 19th century, which permitted discrimination
        - Basis for later SCOTUS victories
    - Democracy: 15th amendment:
      - Pre-15th amendment: Voting rights for white men only
      - Post-15th amendment
        - Voting rights extended to Black men
        - 2,000 Black office holders!
        - Poll taxes, literacy tests, and grandfather clauses limited Black voting
  - Black Codes and Reconstruction
    - Written as a framework to have a legal structure post-slavery. Outlined what Black Americans had a right to do
      - Essentially, just rights to marry and own property
      - Prevented them from owning firearms, entering into contracts, voting, being without a labor contract (white southerners still wanted cheap labor)
    - Congress passes 14th amendment to combat the Black Codes:
      - Anyone who is born in the US (regardless of slave status) is a full citizen eligible for all rights of citizenship and equal protection under the law
  - Overview
    - After Reconstruction, Black Americans had rights to their bodies and labor but not much else
    - Short-lived revolutions in citizenship, voting rights

### Andrew Johnson (1865–1869)

*Historical Figures · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a65680ba8287e0ddcefc0e1e)*

![Andrew Johnson](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e6/Andrew_Johnson_photo_portrait_head_and_shoulders%2C_c1870-1880-Edit1.jpg/1280px-Andrew_Johnson_photo_portrait_head_and_shoulders%2C_c1870-1880-Edit1.jpg)

US President (Democrat)

Favored quick restoration of seceded states to the Union without protection for the newly freed people, and pardoning of ex-Confederates.

Impeached by the House of Representatives in 1868, but acquitted in the Senate by 1 vote

Implemented Presidential Reconstruction to direct the seceded states to form their civil governments. Many states returned their old leaders and passed Black Codes to prevent former enslaved people from voting. Congress tried to overrule the Southern actions, but Johnson vetoed their bills and Congress overrode him.

Opposed the Fourteenth Amendment which gave citizenship to former slaves.

Congress passed Tenure of Office act to restrict his ability to fire Cabinet officials.

Largely ranked one of the worst presidents

### The 13th-15th Amendments (1865–1870)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2a844de2a65680bdac69ec95c08ed8f1)*

Passed while the civil war was still underway. Fought in the South via the Black Codes, which prompted the response of the Civil Act of 1866. Johnson vetoed the Civil Rights Act, and the 14th amendment soon followed to fight the Black Codes.

**Sources**

- [Khan video on the 13th amendment](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/civil-war-era/reconstruction/v/the-reconstruction-amendments-overview-and-13th-amendment)
- [Khan article on life after slavery for African Americans](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/civil-war-era/reconstruction/a/life-after-slavery)

#### Page notes

- 13th Amendment states equality for all Americans
  - Southern states fought the 13th amendment via Black Codes
    - Black Folks not allowed to sue, enter a contract, etc
    - Response was the Civil Act of 1866  (vetoed by Johnson but passed by Congress) and the 14th Amendment
- Life after slavery for Black Folks
  - Emancipation: Promise and Poverty
    - Celebration of newfound freedom; no more whippings, separation of families, ownership
    - However, Blacks still lacked many rights, including right to testify against white folks, serve on juries, etc
    - Required to enter labor contracts under white landowners, and would be arrested and hired out for labor if not doing so
    - Subject to intense poverty, often having to rent land as sharecroppers from white landowners
      - Though Republicans led a movement to give land to former enslaved people, never passed
  - Family, Church, and School became an important central pillar
    - Centers for learning, political organization, and socializing
    - Black ministers were community leaders
    - Establishment of schools and hospitals at all levels
  - Reconstruction (1865-1877)
    - 3 Amendments:
      - 13: ended slavery
      - 14: guaranteed Black citizens the rights associated with American citizenship
      - 15 (1870): guaranteed Black men the right to vote
    - Federal occupation enforced the new amendments in formerly Confederate states
    - The KKK terrorized and killed until in 1871 when their leaders were threatened with arrest and improsonment
    - Military presence gradually withdrawn from 1860-1877
      - Last troops withdrawn in 1877 as part of the Compromise of 1877 (contested presidential race between Rutherford Hayes (R) and Tilden (D))
      - Lynching, disenfranchisement, and segrataionslist laws proliferated until Jim Crow laws finally outlawed in the 1960s
- The 14th Amendment: Basis for citizenship; all persons born or naturalized in the US are citizens and subject to the jurisdiction of the US (ratified in 1868)
  - Section 1 extends to African Americans the same rights that whites enjoyed
    - No state shall make or enforce any law which shall abridge the privileges or immunities of citizens of the US, nor shall any state deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law
    - Wasn’t until the 1960s that the 14th amendment was fully implemented
      - 1920s: Supreme court invokes 1st amendment against states and starts invoking amendments against states
    - Slaughterhouse decision read the “privileges or immunities” clause out of the amendment, “due process” even still controversial in the 20th century
- The 15th Amendment: Right to Vote (ratified in 1870)
  - Necessary because 14th amendment only protected civil rights, not political rights
    - Although Maryland citizen could go to Virginia and make contracts, they can’t participate in Virginia juries or elections
  - In theory, allowed former slaves to vote, but southern states used strategies to prevent them from voting
    - Poll taxes, grandfather clauses, tests that were impossible to pass
    - SCOTUS upheld these laws, and African American voting rights were suppressed until Civil Rights movement of the 1960s
  - Time when Jim Crow rises up, Civil Rights Act struck down, Plessy v Ferguson struck down, Railroad segregation
  - Separate but Equal
    - \~54% of population disagreed with segregation
    - “Allowed” since the separate facilities were “equal”
    - Brown vs Board of Education overturned Plessy vs Fergusson, recognized that segregation is inherently degrading

### Ulysses S. Grant (1869–1877)

*Historical Figures · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a6568062b985ccaf4a2cdebb)*

![Ulysses S. Grant](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5c/Ulysses_S._Grant_1870-1880.jpg/1280px-Ulysses_S._Grant_1870-1880.jpg)

US President (Republican)

Led the Union army to war in the American Civil War.

Prosecuted the Ku Klux Klan, stabilized the national economy, supported congressional Reconstruction, supported the Fifteenth Amendment (states cannot infringe right to vote based on race, color, or previous condition of servitude)

Failed to halt the Long Depression and the Panic of 1873.

Fun facts:   
First president to circumnavigate the globe

### Rutherford B. Hayes (1877–1881)

*Historical Figures · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a656801fb303c3fd2eee783b)*

![Rutherford B. Hayes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/President_Rutherford_Hayes_1870_-_1880.jpg/800px-President_Rutherford_Hayes_1870_-_1880.jpg)

US President (Republican)

Staunch abolitionist who defended refugee slaves in court proceedings. Left politics to fight for the Union, reputed for bravery in combat. Belief in equal treatment without regard to wealth, social standing, or race.

### The Compromise of 1877 (1877)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2e044de2a65680e39e8ed9b222fd8e5e)*

Contested presidential election between Hayes and Tilden; popular vote differed from the electoral vote; standoff for months, and Hayes “allowed” to win via backroom deal if national guard removed from the South

**Sources**

- [Khan Video on the Compromise of 1877 and Plessy v Ferguson](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/south-after-civil-war/v/jim-crow-pt-4)

- [Khan Article on the New South](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/south-after-civil-war/a/the-new-south)

<!-- page-notes:2e044de2a65680e39e8ed9b222fd8e5e -->
#### Page notes

- Contested presidential election: popular vote (D-Tilden) and electoral vote (R-Hayes) differed
  - Backroom deal - Hayes “allowed” to win if the troops enforcing the political system in the South are removed
    - No formal, written agreements resulted from Congress deliberations
    - Congress established a 15-member Electoral commission to resolve the issue of contested states
    - Southern Democrats agreed not to block the vote by which Congress awarded the contested electoral votes to Hayes; Republicans agreed to withdraw federal troops from actively intervening in louisiana and South Carolina politics (the last 2 states occupied by federal troops)
  - Republicans essentially give up ensuring equal racial treatment in the South, after 12 years of federal troop presence
    - Troops are exhausted, people are tired of the conflict
    - 1873 panic - economic bust meaning less spending money for Reconstruction
    - Racism, new labor movement in the North; animated spirit of abolition is falling
      ![This Thomas Nast cartoon, published in 1864, suggested that compromising with the South was tantamount to betraying the Union dead. Courtesy Library of Congress.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/678a0e0d70e4114113dd6d823d23e39ba6efa5f7.jpg)
- Following the Compromise of 1877:
  - Lots of enacted laws - separating public accommodations, voting rights, other Jim Crow laws
  - Directly in contradiction with 14th Amendment; in 1896, Homer Plessy tries to demonstrate this via train demonstration - and separate but “equal” allowed via Supreme Court
  - Marked the end of reconstruction
  - Republicans agreed to home-rule in the South:
    - Republican party would refrain from interfering in the South’s local affairs; letting white Democrats rule
- The End of Reconstruction
  - Effectively ended the Republican Party’s active support of civil rights for Black Americans
  - Southern States rapidly passed laws disenfranchising African Americans and implementing racial segregation
- The New South
  - Rural Agrarian Poverty
    - Sharecropping and tenant farming took the place of slavery and the Southern plantation system
      - Sharecropping/tenant farming: White landlords entered into contracts with impoverished farm laborers to work the land; workers shared a portion of the crop yield with the landlord as payment
      - Landlord typically supplied capital for seed and equipment, sharecropper supplied the labor
    - Since prices on cotton, other crops remained low, sharecroppers and farmers often became indebted
    - Debt peonage: money from selling crops was not enough to pay back loans for seed, tools, living expenses, etc - owed more *after* a year of labor than they had when they started
    - Systems left black and white tenant farmers in dire poverty, and southern economy stagnataed
  - Economic Vision for a new South
    - Henry W Grady: editor of the Atlanta Constitution (newspaper in Georgia capital city)
      - Envisioned broadly expanded manufacturing facilities and commerce
      - During civil war, North rapidly industrialized and advanced technologically (the second industrial revolution); proponents of the New South wanted the southern states to follow the trajectory and remake themselves
      - Vision was primarily for whites; in 1888 address, “I declare… that… the white race must dominate forever in the South”
    - Successes and Failures of the New South
      - Successes
        - Birmingham, Alabama
          - Prospered from iron and steel, mining, furniture production
        - Cigarette rolling machines, American Tobacco Company in NC in 1890
        - Introduction of textile mills; lower wages than for northern workers
      - Failures
        - Success in new industries didn’t tend to support African Americans or poor whites
