US vs Great Britain

Fight over maritime rights, trade restrictions, and British support for Native American Resistance

**Sources**

- [Khan Article on the War of 1812](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-early-republic/x71a94f19:foreign-policy-in-the-early-1800s/a/the-war-of-1812-ush)

<!-- page-notes:22344de2a65680c08d61ef04343248cf -->
#### Page notes

- Origins
  - Tension between the British and the French
    - French supported the US revolution
    - Britain saw France as its main enemy
    - Early 1800s: Napoleonic wars between Britain and France
    - Britain implemented policies to hurt the US, and France by extension
      - Impressment (capture of American vessels and crews)
      - Blocked US trade w/ Europe and ignored its claims of neutrality
  - Britain allied with Native Americans, supporting their resistance
    - Native confederacy led by Shawnee prophet Tenskwatawa and his brother Tecumseh resisted westward expansion
      ![Painting of Tenskwatawa, the Shawnee Prophet, by George Catlin, 1830. Courtesy the Smithsonian American Art Museum.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/f84552b15cd7f7e1b28f4321ad59512900f56f0a.jpg)
    - Protected Canada and westward expansion
  - Disputes over trade, sailors, and settlement led US to declare war in 1812
- Support for and Criticisms of the War
  - Supporters: Henry Clay, John Calhoun
    - Britain was attacking and capturing US crews and ships
    - Britain supported Native American resistance
    - Warhawks: Included Henry Clay, John Calhoun - believed Britain was threatening US independence
  - Critics: Federalists
    - War would hurt US trade and economy
  - Narrow vote to declare war
- War Strategy
  - US to attack Canada to pressure Britain to make peace
  - Army was small, disorganized, poorly equipped
  - Major battles
    - Battle of Thames: Tecumseh was killed and Native American influence in the region collapsed
    - British forces burn down the whitehouse but fail to take Baltimore (where the Star Spangled Banner was written)
      ![Engraving of the burning of Washington, D.C. by the British, by G. Thompson, London, 1814. Library of Congress.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/0e7db260095f3d0adf5932d4af6497b9e2f64ae5.jpg)
  - Other context
    - 1814: Napoleon (French emperor) gives up his throne, allowing more troops to go to North America
    - End of the Napoleonic wars: Britain no longer needed to stop trade with US, so most of the causes for the war were now irrelevant
- The Hartford Convention (December 1814)
  - War of 1812 unpopular in New England (relied on trade, war worsened the economy); blamed Virginia leaders Thomas Jefferson and James Madison
  - Dec 1814: 26 Federalists met in Hartford, CT to discuss concerns
  - Proposals
    - New England to leave the US
  - Widely unpopular, seen as unpatriotic, contributed to the Federalists’ diminishing support
- The Treaty of Ghent
  - By 1815, no clear advantage on either side, both sides tired of fighting, end of Napoleonic wars so no British interest in seizing ships
  - US kept its pre-war territory and Britain kept its territory in Canada
  - Trade Restrictions lifted, prisoners of war returned
  - Began the Era of Good Feelings
    - US-British relations improved
    - Political divisions in US eased
    - New sense of national pride and unity in US
    - War of 1812 considered the “Second War of Independence” since it demonstrated US was strong and independent
      ![Independence Day Celebration in Centre Square, Philadelphia, by John Lewis Krimmel in 1819. Public domain.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-content-images/9aa8dd25c382d2e38e18925d54404860347329c2.jpg)
- The War of 1812 and Native Americans
  - War took a toll on Native Americans
    - 1814 Battle at Horseshoe Bend: General Andrew Jackson defeated the Creek Nation, killing about \~15% of the population
    - Treaty of Fort Jackson: Creeks had to give up 23 million acres and promise to never fight the US again
    - Deaths of Tecumseh and others ended hopes of a Native confederacy
    - British abandoned Native partners post-war
    - Once president, Jackson would lead efforts to remove Native Americans from their land

### Era of Good Feelings (1814–1818)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a65680559c19fd0dce90b337)*

Post-War of 1812, US demonstrated its strength and independence, and was on good terms with Britain. A sense of national pride deepened, and the US drove its own influence in the Americas and in global markets

#### Page notes

- The Monroe Doctrine
  - Context and Overview
    - Monroe declared to Congress that the Americas were no longer open to European colonization
    - 1823: The Monroe Doctrine: Europe should stay out of affairs in the Americas
    - *[Image in Notion: A political cartoon from 1896 showing the influence of the Monroe Doctrine throughout the nineteenth century. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a65680559c19fd0dce90b337)*
  - Enforcement of the Monroe Doctrine
    - Florida under Spanish rule in the early 1800s; in 1819, US acquired Florida via the Adams-Onis Treaty
      - Pressured from General Andrew Jackson’s unauthorized military invasion into area
    - Native Amricans removed from land - forced an estimated 60,000 people from their land
    - Actions both expanded US territory and reinforced the idea that US would use its power to shape the continent to its advantage
- Expanding Trade Markets
  - US trade expanded across the Pacific, to China and newly independent Latin American nations
  - Growing sense of American importance in global trade
  - Push of infrastructure projects: canals, ports, harbors, navy

### Monroe Doctrine (1823)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a65680bda266edd40e21c00f)*

President Monroe declared to Congress that the Americas were no longer available for European colonization. This cemented US independence from Europe, a sense of “ownership” over the land (regardless of Native peoples or Spain/French/British territory control) and incentive to expand

**Sources**

- [Khan video on the Monroe Doctrine](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-early-republic/x71a94f19:foreign-policy-in-the-early-1800s/v/the-monroe-doctrine)

<!-- page-notes:22444de2a65680bda266edd40e21c00f -->
#### Page notes

- Monroe declared to Congress that the Americas should no longer be considered available for European colonization; US would treat it as a threat to their independence and safety
  - Notably, no other powers really responded to that.
  - US didn’t really have an ability to enforce it - limited military prowess
- Became a key facet of American foreign policy in the 19th and 20th centuries
  - Key background for Manifest Destiny
- Historical context
  - 1820: Napoleonic wars over, monarchy reinstated
  - Various movements for independence, particularly in South America
    - US pushing for democracy in South America
  - Post-wars, Spain has higher bandwidth for wars abroad; US concerned that monarchies of Europe might put down the revolutions in South America
- Economic concerns
  - Previous economic system: Mercantilism
    - Colonies work together to enrich the mother country; all trade benefits the country
    - Foreign powers worried that colonies would trade with other powers - bypassing the mother colony
      - Venezuelan, Chilean, Argentinean markets opened up for US and England trade
      - England and US don’t want these nations to return to colonial/mercantilism status
  - George Canning suggests to Adams that the US and Britain go in on a joint pact to stop colonization from Europe
    - Adams didn’t do so - worried that it would seem like US wasn’t strong enough to back itself
    - Adams floated the idea to James Monroe, as a US-specific rather than a joint proposal
- Foreign Concerns
  - Other powers starting to put in footholds - specifically, Russia, Canadian colony expansion
  - Don’t turn new republics into monarchical extensions
- Enforcement
  - US not strong enough to enforce; really relied on the British navy (they wanted neutrality of the seas and trade with new nations)
