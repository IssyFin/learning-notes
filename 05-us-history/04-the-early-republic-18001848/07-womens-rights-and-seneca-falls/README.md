### Early Women’s Rights Movement (1807–1860)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/34344de2a656809d8d35ebb185fda2cc)*

As the industrial revolution and second great awakening shifted the role of women and families, women began to take on a leadership role in society, pushing reform and reflection in labor, abolition, women’s rights, temperance, and more.

#### Page notes

- Women and Work in the early 1800s
  - The Effects of the Industrial Revolution
    - Prior to the Industrial Revolution, most goods were made by hand by artisans and skilled craftsmen, and work was done as a family
      - Families worked together in units under the “Rhode Island system” - whole family would work inside the factory rather than at home (as was done in the putting-out system)
      - Putting-Out system prior to the Rhode Island System: Factory owner supplied raw materials and families completed the work on their own time before returning the finished items
    - Embargo Act in 1807: Americans built factories to produce their own goods
  - The Lowell Girls
    - Francis Cabot Lowell created a new factory system which hired young women rather than whole families; large textile mills in Lowell, Massachusetts
    - Lowell girls worked and lived under strict rules; boarding houses, long hours, few breaks, no talking, and church attendance mandated
    - Wages and a chance for women to live away from home, but difficult work and hours
      - *[Image in Notion: The Lowell textile mill, 1850. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/34344de2a656809d8d35ebb185fda2cc)*
  - Factory work life: 10-12 hours, 6 days a week; difficult and dangerous work, quotas, child labor
    - *[Image in Notion: Women at work spinning textiles at a mill in Alabama, 1911. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/34344de2a656809d8d35ebb185fda2cc)*
  - *[Image in Notion: Image of the young spinners. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/34344de2a656809d8d35ebb185fda2cc)*
- Domestic Life for Women
  - Cult of Domesticity: Many women stayed home and focused on raising families, taught that women were the moral leaders of their households
  - Catharine Beecher (reformer and writer): women had a special talent for teaching
- Minority Women’s work in the era of slavery and Indian removal
  - Enslaved African American women in the South didn’t have these opportunities newly given to white women. Still expected to work long hours in the fields, cook, clean, and care for others, and forced to have children who were born into slavery
  - Native American women pushed west by the Indian Removal Act of 1830; worked to produce food, clothing, and goods for trade to support the family as their lands were taken away
- Women Leadership in Movements
  - Context
    - Cult of domesticity (that women should focus on home and family) helped some women justify greater participation in the public sphere
      - Duty of wives and mothers to protect families from problems like alcohol abuse and poverty
      - Women should help teach moral and democratic values to society as a whole
    - Religious revivals during Second Great Awakening reinforced these goals; women felt they had a moral responsibility to improve their communities
  - Women’s labor movements
    - In search of better treatment in factory jobs, women organized for better treatment
    - 1821: Factory women in Waltham, Massachusetts went on strike to protest wage cuts
    - 1830s: women in Lowell formed the Lowell Factory Girls Association
    - Strikes rarely succeeded, but marked first time many American women spoke out publicly and worked together for change
  - The Abolition Movement
    - The Grimke Sisters (Sarah and Angelina): leading voices for abolition and women’s rights
      - Born into a wealthy slaveholding family, deeply affected by the cruelty; left the South to become Quakers and join the abolitionist cause
      - 1830s: gave public speeches against slavery (shocking at the time, since most people believed it was improper for women to address men in public)
  - Seneca Falls Convention
    - Late 1840s: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucretia Mott, Lucy Stone, and Susan B Anthony key leaders, argued women should have the right to vote
    - 1848: \~300 people (both women and men) gathered at the Seneca Falls Convention in NY, led by Stanton and Mott
      - First major women’s rights convention in the US
    - Wrote and approved the Declaration of Rights and Sentiments
      - Modeled on the Declaration of Independence
      - Listed the injustices women faced and called for legal and social equality.
      - “The history of mankind… is a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, having in direct object the establishment of an absolute tyranny over her.”
  - Republican Motherhood
    - Suggested women had a special role in teaching children about civic duty and moral behavior
      - Catharine Beecher (1845): The Duty of American Women to their Country
        - Argued women could help strengthen the nation by teaching values and self-discipline to the next generation
      - Midde-class belief that women were moral leaders within the household; limited political rights of women but gave them influence as educators and moral guides
- Legacy of the Early Women’s Rights Movement
  - Reform and revival movements of the early 1800s encouraged women to see themselves as capable of shaping the world around them.
  - No “direct” results in legislation, but set the stage for the greater battle
