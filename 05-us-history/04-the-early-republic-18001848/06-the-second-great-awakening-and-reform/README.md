### The Second Great Awakening (1790–1850)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/33944de2a6568026ac1df91e788c7384)*

Major growth in religious practices for Americans, triggered by a need for control with the shift in the economic, informational, and lifestyle “new normal” during the industrial revolution

**Sources**

- [Khan video on the second great awakening](https://youtube.com/watch?v=wnaLjLOAwkc) [Khan video on other causes of the second great awakening](https://youtube.com/watch?v=n_ZyBwiV-Vs)[ Khan video on reform and religious movements ](https://youtube.com/watch?v=LHwqEqOtrBg)

#### Page notes

- Context
  - Evangelical Christians pushed beliefs in “camps” where they would preach on stage - primarily in Appalachia region.
    - Goal to make a “heaven on earth” where Jesus would reign for a thousand years, eager for the apocalypse
    - Famous preachers: Lyman Beecher (father of Harriet Beecher Stowe) and Charles Grandison Finney
    - Movement towards emotional religion vs logical religion
    - Conversion fits
  - Market Revolution
    - Transactions and communication are more widespread - communication isn't in person but by telegraph, and goods aren't to/from immediate community but with the whole world
    - Movement from barter economy  to currency economy
    - Lack of trust in people around you for first time. “Pushing factor” for doing the right thing
    - Family unit labor transitions to waged and individual units
  - Westward expansion=population spread, growth of the “rugged individual” who is in control of their own fate
- Religious movements born out of the second great awakening: Mormons, shakers, Oneida
- Resulting movements
  - Abolitionism
  - Temperence

### Rip Van Winkle (by Washington Irving) (1819)

*Art · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/36c44de2a65680cf947ad8e94cfb0015)*

"Rip Van Winkle" by Washington Irving is a short story published in 1819. It follows a Dutch-American villager who encounters mysterious strangers in the Catskill Mountains, drinks their liquor, and falls into a deep sleep. When he awakens, twenty years have passed and everything has changed—his village is transformed, his friends are gone, and he has slept through the American Revolution. Now an elderly stranger in his own home, Rip must navigate a world he no longer recognizes and reconcile with the life that continued without him.

#### Page notes

- African Americans in the Early Republic
  - In the early 1800s, African Americans were \~15-18% of the US population; most were enslaved but \~430,000 were free.

    | | 1800 | 1850 |
    |---|---|---|
    | Enslaved African Americans | 893,041 | 3,204,313 |
    | Free African Americans | 108,395 | 434,495 |
    | White People | 4,304,480 | 19,553,068 |
    | **Total** | 5,305,925 | 23,191,878 |

  - On rights
    - White men gained more political rights during this period, but Black Americans faced increasing restrictions
    - After 1800, many states passed new laws that limited the rights of both free and enslaved African Americans
  - Life Under Slavery
    - Most enslaved people worked on large plantations, growing cotton (especially important for textile industries in the North and Great Britain), tobacco, and sugar
    - Backbreaking work sunrise-sunset 6 days a week with violence to force faster work
    - Enslaved people treated as property, not as human beings, denied legal rights, faced sexual violence
    - International slave trade technically ended in 1808, but interstate slave trade expanded
  - Resistance and Rebellion
    - Resistance via religion, family, musical, cultural traditions fostered hope
    - Development of African American culture from blended African and Christian beliefs
    - Small acts of daily resistance: breaking tools, working slowly, feigning illness
    - Many risked their lives to run to free states in the North or to Canada (abolished slavery in 1834)
    - Major rebellions
      - 1822: free Black carpenter named Denmark Vesey was accused of planning a large-scale revolt in Charleston, SC; he and 34 others were executed on discovery
      - 1831: Nat Turner (an enslaved preacher) led an uprising in VA that killed 57 white people in carrying out a divine mission to end slavery. Rebellion was put down quickly and white mobs killed or executed over 150 Black people in retaliation
    - State governments in the South responded to uprisings with harsher and harsher restrictions
      - *[Image in Notion: Woodcut dramatizing Nat Turner's rebellion. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/34344de2a65680e4818ac9cdbc3fea62)*
  - Free People of Color and Abolition
    - By 1850, about half of all free African Americans lived in the upper South (MD, VA, NC), many working as sailors, carpenters, domestic servants)
    - In the North, free Black Americans still faced discrimination and poverty, forced to live in poor neighborhoods, denied jobs, and excluded from voting, and banned from living in some areas.
    - Black communities found strength and unity in religion; eg African Methodist Episcopal Church
    - Many free African Americans became leaders in abolition
      - eg David Walker - Boston clothing merchant - wrote An Appeal to the Colored Citizens of the World (1829), Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Turner
