### The Indian Wars (1830–1890)

*Wars · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2f044de2a65680c68b8dddd128495e27)*

A series of wars fought between the US Army and various Native American tribes over forced relocation

**Sources**

- [Khan Article on the Indian Wars and the Battle of Little Bighorn](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/american-west/a/indian-wars)
- [Khan Article on the Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/american-west/a/ghost-dance-and-wounded-knee)

<!-- page-notes:2f044de2a65680c68b8dddd128495e27 -->
#### Page notes

- Indian Removal and the Reservation System
  - Indian Removal Act of 1830 removed numerous Native American tribes from their ancestral lands to designated “Indian territory” west of the MIssissippi
    - Brutal mass migration known as the Trail of Tears
  - Native American resistance to the forced locations became the Indian Wars
    - Seminoles of Florida → Second Seminole War (1835-1842)
  - Indian Appropriations Act of 1851 established Native American Reservations in Oklahoma, Nebraska, and Kansas
    - Was meant to keep Native American tribes off the land that white Americans wanted to settle
- The Indian Wars of the West
  - Manifest Destiny: European Americans were divinely ordained to settle the whole of the North American continent - resulted in surge westward
  - Westward movement resulted in conflict over land and natural resources
    - Gold Rush especially
    - Heated competition in Texas, Arizona, New Mexico, and California
- The Battle of Little Bighorn - considered the beginning of the end of the Indian Wars
  - Context
    - Sioux Wars: 1854-1890
    - 1875: Discovery of goldin the Black Hills region of South Dakota: resulted in miners quickly overtaking Sioux Indian hunting grounds
      - Miners and settlers pled to US Army for protection against the Sioux
    - Great Sioux War of 1876-1877
  - The Battle
    - June 25, 1876: Colonel George Armstrong Custer led battalion in attack on the main Sioux encampment at Little Bighorn
      - Commonly called Custer’s Last Stand
    - Crazy Horse and Sitting Bull led the Native American troops
    - Sioux had more troops and tactical advantage over the US army
      - Defeated the cavalry and killed Custer
      - Abandoned Little Bighorn and fled, fearing further US Army reaction
- The Ghost Dance
  - A spiritual movement foreseeing the banishment of evil from the world
    - Ghost dance ceremony would reunite the spirits of the dead with the spirits of the living to help overtake the white settlers
    - Prophesied the end of white settlement and the return of the buffalo and their way of life.
  - History
    - Solar eclipse on Jan 1, 1889: Wovoka, the shaman of the Northern Paiute tribe claimed that God appeared to him and showed him a land of love and peace
    - Originated in the Northern Paiute tribe but spread to other tribes in the area
    - Chief Sitting Bull (led the Sioux to victory over Custer’s army) embraced the Ghost Dance and facilitated its spread; shot by police officers in December 1890 out of fear that Sitting Bull would flee with adherents of the Ghost Dance
- Massacre at Wounded Knee- Marked the definitive end of the Indian Wars
  - Dec 15, 1890: Chief Sitting Bull is shot by police of fear that he’d flee with Ghost Dance adherents.
  - Dec 29, 1890: US Cavalry surrounded Sioux encampment near Wounded Knee Creek; while attempting to disarm Sioux, shot was fired and a scuffle ensued. US army massacred the Sioux, including hundreds of men, women, and children. The few survivors fled.
  - Official Army inquiry not only exonerated the cavalry, but awarded medals of honor to 20 soldiers.
- Clash of Cultures
  - Perspective of land
    - Native Americans considered land and resources to be communal, to be protected and shared
    - White settlers considered land as personal property
- Other Context
  - Native American Population varied from 2-18 million (pre-European contact) to 237,000 (1900) to around 3 million today.

### The Homestead Act (1862)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680139f4ed276e37d9a23)*

Land-grant legislation that awarded free land to settlers, most of which was already inhabited by Native Americans

**Sources**

- [Khan Article on the Homestead Act](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/x71a94f19:building-the-american-west/a/the-homestead-act-and-the-exodusters)

<!-- page-notes:2fb44de2a65680139f4ed276e37d9a23 -->
#### Page notes

- Context
  - Government land grants a historical practice
    - British encouraged settlement of the “New World” via land grants to settlers
    - Northwest Ordinance of 1787: Established northwest territory (Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Ohio, Wisconsin) and prohibited extension of slavery into that territory - **this tied slavery legality to land-grant legislation**
  - Contentious -
    - Northerners/Republicans: Wanted to free up large plots of land for individual farmers to settle
    - Southerners/Democrats: Wanted to make the lands of the west available only to slave-owners
  - 1860: Homestead Act vetoed by James Buchanan (D), only passed following the secession of Southern states from the Union, signed by Abraham Lincoln
- Details
  - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680139f4ed276e37d9a23)*
  - Homestead \~160 acres awarded to any US citizen who pledged to settle and farm at least 5 years
  - For men at least 21 yo or head of household, who have never borne arms against the US government or given aid/comfort to its enemies
    - Meant ex-Confederate soldiers were ineligible
  - 14th Amendment: Homesteading a possibility for freedpeople!
    - Post Supreme Court decision in 1898, immigrants could apply as well (though, by that time, best land already claimed)
  - 1862-1934: Government granted over 1.5 million homesteads to private citizens (\~10% of the landmass of the US) - massive transfer of land from federal government to individual citizens, inaugurated “land-rushes”
- Exodusters
  - Racial violence and hostile environment for non-whites
    - Vigilante justice at hands of KKK and other white supremacist organizations
    - Many Southern Blacks sought an escape
  - Exodus of 1879: First mass migration of African Americans from the South after the Civil War
    - Migrants known as **exodusters** (name inspired from Exodus in the Bible, where Moses led the Hebrews out of slavery in Egypt into the promised land)
  - Settled in Colorado, Kansas, and Oklahoma
  - Difficult life
    - Many of those who homsteaded the land remained poor
    - Most successful exodusters migrated to urban areas (eg Topeka) for domestic and trade work
    - Kansas had reputation as land of opportunity for Blacks, but many whites still resented their presence and made their lives difficult
    - Exodusters founded several black communities (eg Nicodemus, Kansas)

### The Transcontinental Railroad (1862–1869)

*Invention · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680b68a62f95d5620a580)*

“The West is purely a railroad enterprise”, “annhilate time and space” by linking far-off parts of the country

**Sources**

- [Khan Article on the Trans-Continental Railroad](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/x71a94f19:building-the-american-west/a/the-transcontinental-railroad)

<!-- page-notes:2fb44de2a65680b68a62f95d5620a580 -->
#### Page notes

- 2 Primary companies; 2 lines met in Promontory, Utah (1869)
  - Central Pacific Railroad built eastward from California
  - Union Pacific Railroad built westward from Nebraska
- Government aid to build the railroad:
  - 1862-Pacific Railroad Act - offered significant incentives to railroad companies
    - Cash loans of up to \$48,000/mile of track
    - 175+ million acres of public land (area larger than the state of Texas)
  - Meant to boost the economy - railroad investment became extremely profitable
    - However, this came at a cost to the taxpayer, since railroad companies could keep the benefits: “If there be a profit, the corporations may take it; if there be a loss, the Government must bear it.”
      - *[Image in Notion: Workers on the Central Pacific Railroad about 80 miles from Sacramento. Photograph by Alfred Hart, c. 1862–1869. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680b68a62f95d5620a580)*
- Labor primarily done by immigrants, and dangerous work
  - By 1880, \~2.5% of workers in US employed by railroad companies
  - Primarily Irish and Chinese immigrant workers
  - Labor to dig tunnels, lay track, and act as the “brake man” (climb on top of moving trains to operate the brake, connect train cars by hand)
- Boomtowns and Rail Hubs connected the network of rails
  - Major growth in track: 9,000 miles in 1850 to over 190,000 miles in 1900
    - Included rail lines that linked the coasts
  - Chicago: went from \~200 people in 1833 to city of 1 million by 1890
    - 1893: Hosted the World Fair
    - Connected the farms and ranches of the Great Plains with the markets of the east
- The Cattle Industry
  - Cattle drives from west to east for processing, then refrigerated cars to distribute from there
    - *[Image in Notion: A cattle round-up in Cimarron, a crossroads of the late nineteenth-century cattle drives, c. 1898. Library of Congress.](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680b68a62f95d5620a580)*
    - *[Image in Notion: A group of vaqueros in New Mexico, c. 1890. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/2fb44de2a65680b68a62f95d5620a580)*
  - Cattle herders: \~12,000-40,000 cowboys worked on cattle drives in the late 1800s
    - 1 in 4 was African American, many were Mexican and Mexican American as well
  - Major role in expanding the meatpacking industry, especially in cities like Chicago

### The Dawes Act (1887)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2e944de2a65680318158f18365792c34)*

Broke up reservations into individual plots to force communal Native American tribes into more individual-centric American culture. Signed into law by Grover Cleveland

**Sources**

- [Khan Article on the Dawes Act](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/the-gilded-age/american-west/a/the-dawes-act)

<!-- page-notes:2e944de2a65680318158f18365792c34 -->
#### Page notes

- Overview
  - Authorized government to break up reservations into individual plots. Only individuals who took the plots were granted US Citizenship
  - Aimed to assimilate Native Americans into US culture
  - Over 90 million acres stripped from Native Americans and given to non-natives
- Context
  - Most white Americans didn’t think they could coexist with Native Americans - very different cultures and practices
  - Whites wanted the land - US instituted the reservation system in 1851 to restrict western land to white expansion
  - Continued expansion resulted in white Americans feeling that Native Americans had to assimilate into American culture so that they could coexist
  - Native Americans resisted the takeover, via the Indian Wars
    - US military ultimately subdued Native Americans and forced them onto reservations
- Provisions and Effects
  - Explicitly sought to destroy social cohesion of Native American culture and force them to disavow their traditions
  - Tribal lands parceled out into individual plots; only those who accepted the individual plots were granted US citizenship
  - Remaining land sold to white Americans
- Amendments
  - Originally, didn’t apply to the “5 civilized tribes” (Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Creek, and Seminole) - amended to include those protected tribes in 1898 via Curtis Act
    - Called civilized because they already adopted many elements of American culture
    - Protected by treaties, had been guaranteed their lands would be free of white settlers
  - Great Depression: FDR brought back the Reservation System via the US Indian Reorganization Act - authorized the tribes to reform their tribal governments
