### The Gilded Age (1865–1898)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2bc44de2a65680bf8df1e2e15c17de64)*

A period of rapid industrialization and urbanization in the US. Though it featured a veneer of prosperity, in truth the wealth divide was vast, and the isolated wealth built off of corruption and immigrant labor.

<summary>Overview</summary>

- Period: 1865-1898
  - 1865: End of the Civil War
  - 1898: Start of the Spanish American War
- Gilded Age: Coined by Mark Twain in 1890
  - A play on the term “Golden Age” - referenced the thin veneer of “prosperity” hiding the cheap tin beneath
- Intense Industrialization
- Severe wealth disparities and political corruption under guise of prosperity
  - Notable barons
    - Andrew Carnegie (Steel)
    - JP Morgan (Banking)
    - John D Rockefeller (Oil)
  - Wealth mostly gained through bribery and kickbacks, built on backs of immigrant laborers
  - Severe wealth disparity
    - Jacob Riis published photos on how impoverished lived in lower Manhattan, revealing the poor conditions in tenements (hastily constructed apartments in the backyards of other buildings)
- Mass immigration to US
  - Most immigrants ended up impoverished in cities

</details>

<summary>America Moves to the City</summary>

- Urbanization
  - Prior, most folks lived on farms:
    - 1790: 9/10 of people lived in rural areas
    - 2025: 1/50 people live in rural areas
    ![](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-content-images/bed49ddbaef3fb6d29e95d765b914dfeb3677836.png)
- Driving forces of urbanization
  - Industrialization (growth of factories and machine-based production)
    - Made new jobs
    - Changed living and working conditions
      ![](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-content-images/5876249e5ab8aedcc0724814458a9a66ec5169b4.jpg)
  - Immigration
    - Europe → US
  - Immigration from Europe coupled with rural → urban movement considered one of the largest mass migrations in human history
    ![](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-content-images/3b2b7b8c4e9004012a4dcd9aa86ba7deeb9925f0.jpg)
- Differences between the 1st (late 1700s-early 1800s) and 2nd (late 1800s) Industrial Revolutions
  - 1st Industrial Revolution:
    - Movement to machinery rather than Handmade
    - Included the cotton gin, steam engine, telegraph
  - 2nd Industrial Revolution
    - Interchangeable parts, assembly lines
    - Included the telephone, electric lights, automobiles
    - Moved Americans to the city for factory work
- Growth of the City
  - Invention of bessamer process (made steel cheaper) and elevators made it possible for cities to grow upward via skyscrapers
  - Invention of trolley cars improved transportation, allowing cities to grow outwards. Also included the rise of suburbs
  - With rise of immigration and continued urbanization, ethnic enclaves formed where people from the same background lived and worked in close quarters
- Poverty in the City
  - Poor families often packed into tenements, which were unsafe, crowded structures lacking clean air and water and plumbing
  - Poor living and sanitary conditions gave rise to diseases including typhoid fever, tuberculosis, and cholera among impoverished conditions
  - Jacob Riis published series of photos titled "how the other half lives", documenting the poor conditions that impoverished folks suffered under
    ![](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-content-images/b1d4e058d87c519d7c57df15ae82726c7e53590b.jpg)

<summary>The 2nd Industrial Revolution</summary>

- Materials
- Steel
  - Steel may be the most important material of the gilded age
    - Pittsburgh key for the 2nd Industrialization (need steel for railroads, buildings, never-ending boom)
      - Chicago also important here)
  - Invention of the Bessamer Process: Makes steel faster and cheaper
  - Grows the US *out*
    - Lay 40,000 miles of new tracks of rail; *lots* of steel needed
      - Entire country now connected by rail; more rail in the US than all of Europe combined
      - Enables the US to move out, connect markets (specifically, raw materials of west - gold and cattle - to the cities for processing)
  - Grows the US *up*
    - Skyscrapers are now an option
    - Invention of the Odis elevator
- Refrigeration
  - Allows more efficient and frequent transport, connection of markets
- Telephone
  - Allows communication between businesses to take off; more efficient and cooperation
  - Web of markets
  - Far more people working for other people (rather than themselves) than ever before
- Timetables
  - Rise of timekeeping between areas since rail needs connected timetracking
- Electricity
  - Though not invented in the Gilded Age, became a critical tool
  - Amount of sleep following the invention of the lightbulb: 9hrs → 7hrs
  - Allowed longer operating hours, reduced risk of fire
- Misc
  - Things can be made faster and cheaper, and can also be done with less skilled labor

<summary>Development of the Middle Class</summary>

- Wages and working conditions
  - Blue-collar jobs:
    - Most residents of cities worked demanding jobs for low wages, poor working and living conditions
  - White-collar jobs
    - Industry also required bookkeepers, managers, secretaries, etc for business purposes
    - Clerical jobs generally open to women as well as men; fostered growth of a *middle class* of educated office workers
    - Tended to spend surplus income on growing variety of consumer goods and leisure activities
    - Typically reserved for white, native-born employees
  - Women in white-collar work
    - Smaller hands ideal for typewriters and telephone switchboards
    - Smaller paychecks saved employers money
    - Allowed new freedom and independence as wage-earners
- Wealth disparity
  - Government’s *laissez-faire* policies meant that those with wealth could become titans of industry
    - 1890: 1% of the population controlled 25% of the wealth in the US
  - Rise of debate regarding what the aristocrats owed to the rest of society
    - Andrew Carnegie’s “Gospel of Wealth”: promoted the idea that, during their lifetimes, the rich should give away their money to benefit the public
      - “The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth” (Carnegie)
      - Argued that great inequalities of wealth were a natural consequence of industrial society; and the wealthy should engage in phianthropy to make sure that the deserving poor could improve themselves
      - Rather than living ostentatiously, leaving money to heirs, or willing their fortunes after death, they should donate while still alive
      - “…the man of wealth thus becoming the mere agent and trustee for his poorer brethren…”
        ![The Carnegie Free Library of Braddock in Braddock, Pennsylvania, built in 1888. Wikimedia Commons](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/0a14d7e93e5fae3f0645c8db39d4bfafc85610bf.jpg)
      - Criticisms of Carnegie:
        - Used charitible giving to justify careers of corruption and building monuments to themselves
        - Ruthlessly cut wages, lengthened shifts, accepted dangerous working conditions, and undermined unions at his steel mills
        - 1892 Homestead strike, staged by steelworkers attempting to prevent a pay cut, led to the deaths of several strikers and strikebreakers
    - Some thinkers of the time believed it would actually harm society for the rich to lift up the poor - Social Darwinism - and prevent the human race from evolving
    - William Graham Sumner: What do Social Classes owe each other? Not a thing
- Jobs and Education
  - Expansion of higher education
    - 1862: Morrill Land Grant Act: Gave federal lands for the purpose of building public Universities
    - Previously reserved for the children of elite; now open to a new class of young people
    - Open to men and women
- Leisure and Consumption
  - Rise of department stores (Macy’s, Marshall Field’s) to offer ready-made clothes and household goods to spend newly-available extra cash
    - Would previously be made at home
  - Leisure Activities became more available and popular
    - Theatre and Vaudeville
    - Amusement parks
    - Circuses
    - Dances
    - Sporting Events (like Baseball!)
      ![The New York Gothams in 1884. During the Gilded Age, the first professional baseball leagues formed. The Gothams later became the New York Giants, and then the San Francisco Giants. Wikimedia Commons](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/da4fdacf5da9d42d5665b5e7119aa0db2cc7eb5f.jpg)

<summary>Social Darwinism</summary>

- Origin of term
  - Charles Darwin wrote *On the Origin of Species* in 1859 to describe the process of evolution through natural selection;
  - Sociologists and others adopted the term to describe their own ideas about the human social world; misapplied it to explain the divide between the rich and the poor, as well as differences and cultures
- Stated that businessmen and others who were economically/socially successful was because they were biologically and socially the fittest, and deserved their wealth
  - The poor were  “naturally” weak and unfit, and by extension, shouldn’t be allowed to survive/propagate
  - However, it completely misapplied the theory of evolution, attributing to skill and talent what could more truly be attributed to inherited resources, abuse of systems in place, and pure chance.
- A similar false application of economic darwinism was applied to theories of race, racism, and eugenics, and generally had an impact on how people were treated in society
  - Claimed national superiority of one people over another
  - Strongly influenced immigration law
  - Many pointed to Darwinism as an argument against government programs aiding the poor (”natural inferiority” should be bred out of the human population)
  - Inspired the eugenics movement of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, which sterilized individuals considered “feeble-minded” or “unfit”, disproportionately targeting minorities, women, and immigrants, and continued in the US until the 1970s
    - Eugenics only lost itsp opularity after WWII and association with Nazi racial propaganda
- Effect of Social Darwinism on Immigration and Imperialism
  - Gilded Age was a time of massive immigration; white Anglo-Saxon American viewed these newcomers as inferior
    - Less likely to speak English, and more likely to be Catholic or Jewish rather than Protestant, than earlier immigrants
    - Considered by many white americans as “less evolved” than immigrants from England, Ireland, or Germany
  - Social Darwinism used as a justification for American Imperialism in Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines following the Spanish-American War (duty of white Americans to bring civilization to “backwards” peoples)
- Historian’s Perspective
  - Note that Herbert Spencer, not Darwin, coined “Survival of the Fittest” as an explanation for disparities in society:
    - Why imperialist nations people tend to be better off than the folks they colonized
    - Why government shouldn’t aid the needy
  - People tend to take a worldview that fits one thing and try to apply it to everything else they encounter
  - Other reasons why Social Darwinism doesn’t make sense
    - Any time you’re trying to remove variation from a population,  you’re moving away from evolution, and are removing possibilities for adaptation for the future - results in a weaker population
      - Variation prepares a population for an uncertain future!
    - Can’t confuse culture for biology - if we consider a cultural element as “inferior”, that’s not really accurate - just a tell that it’s considered “different” for us.
      - Note that, on the same side of the coin, colonism was justified by the “white man’s burden” to “help these other races evolve”
  - As a side note, race isn’t really a “thing” reflected in genetics. Some superficial traits are inherited that we consider to be markers of “race”, but there’s so much variation that it’s not really a separation. An arbitrary way to categorize people.
- Biologist’s Perspective
  - white Americans considered races in humanity as “on a spectrum” from least to most “evolved”, putting white eastern Europeans (themselves) as the “most evolved”
  - Even getting past the racism of it, that’s not even how biological evolution works - it’s not a “gradient” and more of a branching from a common ancestor. All organisms have been evolving from the same point. “More evolved” isn’t really a thing.
  - 3 key ingredients of “natural selection”:
    - Variation
    - Heritable differences
    - Advantageous differences given the environment
  - Biological principles can’t really be applied to society, as neatly as the theory seems to fit - it’s a falsety people want to consider true, and seems scientific even if it’s not based on anything besides self-importance

<summary>Politics</summary>

- Dichotomy between participation and power
  - Highest voter participation
  - Major parties were entrenched in corruption and scandal
    - Distributing govt jobs to supporters
    - Using govt funds for personal spending
- Spoils system strong since Jackson’s administration
- The Republican Party
  - Primarily included rural farmers from north and west, African Americans, and white anglo-saxon protestants
  - “Vote as you shot” wrt Civil War
  - Worked to promote racial equality, but effort waned by the late 1870s
  - Promoted expansion of business and infrastructure
    - Subsidies and land grants to railroad industry
    - Protective tariffs
    - Tie USD to gold standard, benefitting banks and businessowners
- The Democratic Party
  - Promoted state and local control of government
  - Opposed tariffs
  - Valued personal liberty over moral reform
  - Popular with white southerners and northern urbanites, especially Irish and German immigrants
  - Imposed segregation and Jim Crow laws
  - Distributed food and jobs to the poor in return for votes
    - eg Tammany Hall in NYC, w/ William Boss Tweed
      - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2bc44de2a65680bf8df1e2e15c17de64)*
- The People’s Party (The Populists)
  - Emerged in the 1890s from cooperative farmers organizations that emerged after the civil war
  - Represented Farmers - upset by the plummeting of agriculture prices due to mechanization, high prices for shipping due to unregulated railroads, and difficulty paying back loans due to hard money policy (from the gold standard)
  - Free silver - coin silver in addition to gold to increase money supply and promote inflation
    - Decrease money’s value and make it easier to pay back loans
  - Successful until Democratic Party incorporated free silver into its platform

</details>

<summary>Patronage and the Spoils System</summary>

- Politics corrupt, backroom deals
  - Eg compromise of 1877- resolved presidential dispute of 1876 by awarding presidency to Rutherford Hayes (who lost the popular vote) in exchange for removal of troops from South following Civil War
  - Every president from 1876 to 1892 won the presidency despite losing the popular vote
  - Lots of weak presidents, incumbents rarely elected
    - Populace preferred the devil they *didn’t* know to the one they *did*
    - Presidents had barely enough power to pay back favors to those who put them in office - and doing so became their focus while in office
- The Spoils System: The power of the president to practice widespread patronage
  - Name friends and supporters to various political posts
- Debates over Civil Service Reform and the Liberal Republican Party
  - Allowed reform-minded Republicans to break free and join the democratic party
  - Horace Greeley (newspaper editor) selected as candidate
  - Lost in the election, but set the stage for pursuing reform
- Hayes’ Presidency (1877-1881)
  - Control of the President
    - Roscoe Conkling (leader of the Stalwarts, who pushed maintaining the spoils system) and James Blaine (leader of the Half-Breeds, who were named so because they were “half-Republican” and wanted a modicum of control over the spoils system) pressured Hayes for change in opposite directions
  - Panic of 1873 - an economic panic caused by Hayes stymying progress towards African American civil rights and halting the coinage of silver
  - In removing Chester A Arthur from his post (he frequently used his position to gain political favors), Hayes lost popularity with the Half-Breeds
  - With compromise of 1877, Hayes lost much of the rest of his popularity and failed to secure reelection
  - Successor picked as a compromise candidate between the Half-Breeds and the Stalwarts: James A Garfield (w/ vice president Chester A Arthur)
    - Garfield then assassinated by a “Stalwart of Stalwarts”, leading to the presidency of Chester A Arthur, who promptly distanced himself from the stalwarts
- Chester A Arthur, despite his previous policies, used his newfound presidency to push civil service reform:
  - Pendleton Service Act of 1883, which created the Civil Service commission, setting aside 15% of civil service jobs to be selected via a competitive civil service examination process

</details>

<details>
<summary>Laissez-Faire Policies</summary>

- Influential Americans pushed a “Laissez-Faire” approach to government, where the government should allow the market and society to operate without government intervention or oversight.
  - Limited regulation, limited support for workers, and limited protections for formerly enslaved people
- Pushed as a result of belief in “Social Darwinism” - being poor was a personal failure and not something the government should try to fix.
- Some resisted laissez-faire ideas:
  - American workers organized their own unions, held strikes, and demanded better working conditions
  - Lucy and Albert Parsons - an interracial couple who escaped Texas, moved to Chicago and organized the Knights of Labor - one of the first national labor unions
    - Included skilled and nonskilled workers, men and women, African Americans and whites
    - Pushed for government reform
- Government frequently sided with laissez-faire ideals:
  - Lochner v New York (1905) - Supreme Court struck down a law that limited bakeries to a 10-hour workday on justification of “liberty of contract”- workers and employers should be free to make contracts without government influence
  - Turned away from reconstruction -
    - After Civil War, US troops were used to protect rights of Black Americans in the South
    - 1870s, gov’t decided that they shouldn’t interfere and let the market and society play itself out, claiming Black Americans should be free to succeed or fail without US help

</details>

<summary>Rise of Labor Unions</summary>

- Context
  - In the 1800s, America shifted from a nation of small farmers to a nation of industry
    - For the first time in US history, more people worked for other people for wages than for themselves as farmers or craftsmen
  - In early days of industry, American government played little to no role in regulation
    - Monopolies dominated industry, keeping prices high
    - Industries kept wages low and bribed judges/Congress to side with them in disputes
  - Business owner power at an all time high
- Labor Unions
  - Sought to reconcile disparity in resources between large businesses and individual workers to improve working conditions
    - Bargain with businessowners for higher wages, shorter hours, better working conditions, and union recognition
    - Power in striking: where workers refuse to work (and prevent others from working in their place) leaving factories and mills idle
      - Businesses can fight strikes by firing workers, hiring short-term workers (scabs)
- The Knights of Labor
  - A union founded in 1869
  - Goals
    - 8-hour workday for laborers
    - Vision in which workers, not capitalists, owned industries in which they labored
    - End child labor
    - End convict labor
  - Welcomed unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled workers, imm igrants, african americans, and women
    - Support from coalminers in 1870s and 1880s
    - Support from railroad workers
      - 1885 strike against Wabash Railroad
- The Haymarket Square Riot
  - May 4, 1886
  - Rally in support of 8-hour workday in Chicago’s Haymarket Square
    - Included anarchists
  - Dynamite bomb was thrown, and in mayhem, seven policemen and four citizens were killed
  - 8 anarchists charged with preaching incendiary doctrines, sentenced to long prison terms or death
    - No evidence tying them to the bombing
  - Following the riot, Knights of Labor associated with anarchism and violence
    ![Engraving depicting the Haymarket Square riot, published in Harper's Weekly in 1886. Courtesy Chicago Historical Society.](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/e11368075de83b90094ff271283340a5e4273686.jpg)
- American Federation of Labor
  - Skilled workers fled the Knights of Labor to join the American Federation of Labor (AFL)
    - Primarily craft unions, skilled workers, eg carpenters, stonemasons, and printers
  - Focused on higher wages, shorter hours, better working conditions
- Unions still relatively small scale - by 1900, only 3% of working people belonged to a union.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Immigration and migration in the Gilded Age</summary>

- Massive movement to cities
  - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2bc44de2a65680bf8df1e2e15c17de64)*
  - Immigration between 1880-1920:
    - \~23 million immigrants arrived, primarily from Eastern and Southern Europe
    - Expansion of NY, Chicago, San Francisco
    - Cultural challenges in maintaining traditions privately while integrating American norms publicly
      - *[Image in Notion: Immigrants arriving at Ellis Island in New York City, 1915. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/2bc44de2a65680bf8df1e2e15c17de64)*
  - “Native-born American” response
    - Period was a conscious effort on part of “native-born Americans” to define what was considered to be “American”
    - Attach some explanation to the divide between wealth classes, and maintain sense of racial and cultural superiority - social darwinism
  - Some groups supported immigration via “Settlement Houses”
    - eg Hull House in Chicago (Jane Addams)
    - Offered language instruction, vocational training, childcare
    - “The essence of democracy is its assurance that every individual should have the chance to realize their potential” (Jane Addams)
  - Exchange in ideas:
    - Fusion of old and new ideas via food, music, and language
  - Mexican Immigrants
    - Context: Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)
      - Mexican Americans were granted opportunity to become American citizens at the end of the Mexican-American war, but second-class citizenship
    - Second-class citizenship
      - Mexican Americans displaced by white settlers, demands for legal redress ignored
      - Relegated to poor paying jobs; primarily peons (manual laborers similar to slaves), vaqueros (cattle herders) and cartmen, as well as hazardous mining tasks
        - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/2bc44de2a65680bf8df1e2e15c17de64)*
      - Often outvoted, outnumbered, and outcast politically
        - Legal disputes, land disputes, law enaction
      - Discriminated against w/ employment, legally, socially, and isolated
    - Some fought back against these conditions
      - 1889-1890: New Mexico: las Gorras Blancas (the White Caps)
        - Group of several hundred Mexican Americans
        - Fought to reclaim land and intimidate white americans to prevent further land seizures
        - Raided white farms, burning homes, barns, and crops
          - didn’t result in any fundamental changes
        - Frequently captured, beaten, imprisoned, and remainders grew up
    - Laws passed to deprive Mexican Americans of heritage and land
      - Sunday Laws: Prohibited “noisy amusements” like bullfights, cockfights, and other cultural gatherings
      - Greaser Laws: Permitted imprisonment of any unemployed Mexican American on charges of vagrancy
  - Chinese Immigrants
    - Context
      - Chinese immigrants arrived in great numbers at end of 1800s, majority in California working menial jobs
    - Chinese immigrants in the American West
      - Immigration largely spurred by the gold rush
        - 1849: Barely 650 Chinese immigrants in the US
        - 1852: Over 25,000
        - 1880: Over 300,000 (primarily in California)
      - Many found employment building the first transcontinental railroad, some worked in cotton plantations in the south following the civil war
      - Many booked ticket to US using a “credit-ticket” - US businessmen paid passage, immigrants were then indebted for a period of work
      - Most Chinese immigrants were men; by 1890, less than 5% of the Chinese population in the US was female
      - Though few intended to stay in the US long term, many were forced to do so as they were financially unable to return home
    - Chinese immigration and citizenship
      - 1790 law prevented Chinese immigrants from obtaining US citizenship through naturalization
      - Harsh discrimination and violence from American settlers in the west
      - Taxes to pay to participate in the gold rush, subsequent forced relocation into Chinese districts
      - Chinese community banded together to create social and cultural centers (eg in San Francisco)
      - Anti-Chinese discrimination resulted in “anti-coolie clubs” (slur directed towards people of Asian descent) and violent protests
    - 1885 - Rock Springs, Wyoming: riot resulted in over two dozen Chinese immigrants being murdered by whites, and many more injured
    - 1879 - Chinese Exclusion Act
      - First significant law restricting immigration into the US
      - Restricted immigration from China for ten years; extended on multiple occasions until its repeal in 1943
