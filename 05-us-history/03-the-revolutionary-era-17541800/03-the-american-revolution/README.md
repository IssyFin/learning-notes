#### Page notes

- Boston Massacre and Boston’s role as the Cradle of Revolution
  - Culture had long developed resentment of British rule
    - Lots of poverty, high unemployment
  - 1768: Britain sends soldiers to Boston to enforce taxes
    - Tense; similar to a military occupation (\~1/3 of population was a British soldier)
    - Frequent street fights and violent tensions
  - Boston Massacre (1770)
    - Britain orders removal of all residents from the Boston Manufactory House (shelter for the poor, ill, and homeless) so soldiers could move in
    - The people resisted and were successful!
    - March 1770: One such confrontation - group of angry townspeople surrounded the British soldiers and threw sticks and rocks. British fired back, killing 5 and wounding others.
    - First casualty of the war: Crispus Attucks, a sailor of African and Native American descent
    - Local newspapers mourned those killed in the massacre, and the event was used as fuel for the revolution, particularly by Paul Revere and Samuel Adams
    - Aftermath of the Boston Massacre
      - Colonists gave the redcoats a fair trial; all but 2 found not guilty
        - *[Image in Notion: Paul Revere's engraving of the Boston Massacre, 1770 (Library of Congress)](https://app.notion.com/p/22344de2a656808d9476d187297431d0)*
- The Tea Act
  - 1773: British Prime  Minister Lord North introduced the Tea Act
  - Goal was to rescue the struggling East India company (governed large parts of India); gave the company a massive loan (1.5 million) and gave exclusive rights to sell to the American colonies
  - Act technically lowered the price of tea, but was seen by the colonists as a means of control
- The Boston Tea Party
  - Britain authorized shipment of thousands of pounds of tea to Boston. Colonists feared that accepting the shipment would signal them accepting Parliament’s right to tax them
  - Governor Hutchinson insisted that the ships dock and the tax be paid by the merchants.
  - Sons of Liberty (secret group organized in 1765) organized the protest - dumped 342 crates of tea (about \~\$10,000) into the harbor disguised as Native Americans
    - *[Image in Notion: Nathaniel Currier, print depicting the Boston Tea Party, 1846. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/22344de2a656808d9476d187297431d0)*
  - News of tea party spread, and at other harbors (eg New York), colonists wouldn’t let ships unload their cargo
- The Coercive Acts/The Intolerable Acts
  - Harsh laws in retribution for the Boston Tea Party
  - Shut down Boston’s port, suspended the colony’s legislature (removing their right to self government), sent troops to occupy the city, and forced quartering of troops in homes, inns, etc
  - In response, colonial leaders called for a meeting of the colonies; resulted in the 1774 First Continental Congress, where a boycott of British goods was organized
- The First Continental Congress
  - Colonies met in Philadelphia to discuss response to British policies
  - Most colonies agreed on boycotting British goods, but division on whether to stop exporting as well; Southern economies depended on the income from those exports
    - Decided to stop exports in one year’s time, to allow the colonies time to adjust
  - 1774: Issued the Declaration of Colonial Rights and Grievances
    - Denied Britains right to tax the colonies
    - Condemned stationing of troops in Boston
    - Described Intolerable Acts as an attack on freedom
    - *Did* reaffirm loyalty to King George III and accepted Britain’s authority to regulate trade - indicated that the colonies were open to repairing relations w/ Britain
  - Delegates decided to meet again in 1775 if conditions didn’t improve
- The Beginning of the American Revolution
  - Massachusetts is officially in rebellion February 1775
    - British troops patrolled streets, preparing to seize weapons stored by colonel militias in nearby towns
    - Patriots organized network of spies to track British movements and ready their defenses
    - Gage sent 700 soldiers to march to Concord, where Patriots were believed to store guns/ammo
  - The Midnight Riders: Paul Revere, Samuel Prescott, and William Dawes would ride through Massachusetts to warn when soldiers were on the march
    - Also spread the news that British were heading to Lexington, possibly to arrest Samuel Adams and John Hancock - two of the most prominent Patriot leaders
  - The Shot Heard Round the World
    - April 19, 1775: British soldiers arrived at Lexington, where 80 militiamen waited on the village green
    - Patriot Captain John Parker: “Stand your ground; don’t fire unless fired upon, but if they mean to have a war, let it begin here”
    - Standoff, until a shot rang out (no one knows who fired it) and the British charged. One British soldier and 8 militiamen were lost
    - The British troops continued to Concord, where hudnereds of armed colonists waited at the North Bridge
      - Gunfire was exchanged and the British retreated, shortly thereafter caught in a gauntlet and attacked behiund wstone walls and trees
    - Marked the first colonist victory
      - *[Image in Notion: Plate II. A view of the town of Concord, 1775 (New York Public Library Digital Collections)](https://app.notion.com/p/22344de2a656808d9476d187297431d0)*
  - The attack on Boston: Militias from other colonies rushed to support Massachusetts, trapping British troops in Boston
- The American Revolution
  - Context
    - British Empire was the most powerful in the world, with huge navy, trained army, and money+resources
    - Colonies were loosely united with little authority, no standing army/navy, relied on militias and volunteer soldiers w/ little training and few supplies
    - Ideas were the lifeblood of the revolution; Enlightenment principles, belief that people should govern themselves.
    - Some colonists still loyal to the British crown (About 1 in 5) - typically merchants, Anglican clergy, and recent immigrants
      - British tried to improve their numbers by incentivizing Americans to join their ranks - promised freedom to enslaved people who fought for the British
  - Colonist Strategy
    - Resistance efforts from ordinary people were essential
      - Local militias (Part-time soldiers, volunteers)
    - Small group strategies, ambush, gather intelligence/sabotage as civilians
    - Familiarity with the territory
    - Survival over attack; keep army intact and avoid unnecessary losses; wear down over time
    - Grassroots military resistance - local militias, skirmishes, and sabotaging
  - The Continental Congress created the Continental Army in 1775, led by George Washington
    - *[Image in Notion: Washington Crossing the Delaware by Emanuel Leutze, 1851. Public domain.](https://app.notion.com/p/22344de2a656808d9476d187297431d0)*
  - Major battles
    - Focus on surprise attacks; eg attack on Trenton when crossing the Delaware
      - These wins didn’t win the war but demonstrated that the Continental Army could fight and win
    - Battle of Saratoga in upstate NY: disrupted British plans to divide the colonies along the Hudson and convinced France to ally with the US; turning point in the war
    - 1781: Battle at Yorktown: American and French forces coordinated attack on the British (Washington and French General Rochambeau on land campaign, while French navy blocked British escape by sea) - British General Cornwallis surrendered - last major battle of the war
  - Alliances and Support
    - Following the Battle of Saratoga, France entered the conflict - American victory at Saratoga showed them that a Patriot win was possible
    - France provided troops, weapons, funds, and a navy; Spain and the Dutch Republic also provided assistance
- The Second Continental Congress
  - Organized by 13 colonies in Philadelphia to decide how to organize the war effort and what kind of government to pursue
    - Prior, the colonies had no single army - only small militias organized by individual towns and colonies
  - 2 main political groups: Conservatives (eg John Jay of NY and John Dickinson of PA), who wanted to make peace with Britain, and Radicals (eg John Adams of MA and Thomas Jefferson of VA), who believed colonies should move to complete independence
  - Olive Branch Petition - July 5 1775 - pledged loyalty to King George III and asked him to restore the colonies’ rights and stop fighting
    - Next day - Congress issued the Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms - explained why colonies had been forced to fight for their freedoms - sent a mixed message to the British government
    - British rejected the Olive Branch Petition
  - Following Lexington and Concord and the Siege of Boston, many delegates lost faith in reconciliation and joined the radicals
  - Second Continental Congress started to act more like a national government; sent ambassadors, printed paper money, organized the army
    - However, could not levy taxes or control trade - financing the war therefore a difficult endeavor
  - July 4, 1776: Approved the Declaration of Independence (stated colonies’decision to form a new nation based on liberty and equality)
    - First draft by Thomas Jefferson - focus on natural rights and government by consent
  - Considerations for new nation’s government
    - How to make a government strong enough to function but not too powerful
    - What rights should citizens have
    - How can states share power with the national government
  - Articles of Confederation: November 15, 1777 - unicameral legislature (one governing body), while each state keeps most of its power and independence (except for war/peace/foreign relations) - constitution from 1781-1788
- Women in the Revolution
  - Gender roles transitioned from domestic to heavily involved
    - Prior, women focused on raising children, managing households, and supporting the family farm/business; private and separate from politics
    - When men fought, women took on the rest of the responsibilities - managed property, ran the business, stepped into the public sphere
  - The Daughters of Liberty: Formed following the Stamp Act in 1765
    - Women showed their desire for American independence by refusing to buy British goods, making their own instead
    - Homespun cloth was a public statement of independence - held demonstrations to spin thread together and demonstrate that colonies could be self-sufficient and independent from britain
  - Supported revolutionary cause by sewing uniforms and blankets, making bullets, raised money for war. Also traveled with army to cook, clean, mend, heal, carry water, spied
    - Molly Pitcher - manned William Hays’ cannon after his fall; her first job on battlefield was carrying pitchers of water to soldiers
