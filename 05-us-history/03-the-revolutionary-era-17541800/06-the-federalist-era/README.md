#### Page notes

![](https://cdn.kastatic.org/ka-perseus-images/7e69118a16c3c4429b6e67c274c823dfdc4f4ff4.jpg)

- Context
  - First president following the ratification of the Constitution in 1789
  - Would set precedent for which problems president would address and how they would be solved
  - Actions established a strong central government
  - Generally well understood he’d be the first president
    - Not because he was a general, but had an “inner virtue”- would know how far to go
    - Much done in secret; others took notes and wrote after members were dead
  - Very nervous that the new government would be successful; believed strongly that power corrupts, followed rigid moral code and believed standards were necessary for behavior. Wrote list of a hundred things for a gentleman to follow to be a proper human
- Farewell address
  - Advised against political parties
    - Political parties emerged very soon after - late 1790s brought the Federalists and the Democratic Republicans.
  - Advised against political factions based on geography
  - Advised against long-term relationships with other countries
- Debate over the national bank
  - US national debt high following Revolutionary War; soldiers weren’t being paid and money needed to be paid back to France. Not clear whether the states or federal government should be responsible.
  - Alexander hamilton proposed a 2-part solution: federal government assumes debts but can create a national bank to print federally backed money
  - National Bank was technically not discussed in the Constitution; however, Hamilton believed the “necessary and proper” aspect of Article I gave those provisions; Thomas Jefferson believed that was a misinterpretation of the clause and considered a national bank to be an absue of power.
- The French Revolution and the Proclamation of Neutrality
  - French revolution followed shortly after the American revolution;  when France asked US for assistance, tricky decision whether to stay neutral, since France was major support of US during the American Revolution
  - US neutrality primarily motivated by the lack of resources and massive debt following the Revolutionary War
- The Whiskey Rebellion
  - To pay off post-war debt, Alexander Hamilton proposed a tax on whiskey; farmers starkly against tariffs since their economy depended heavily on whiskey
  - Tax collectors met with violence, and Washington combatted the resistance with troops
  - No fighting officially broke out post troop deployment, but the incident reenforced that the federal government could use funds to put down civil unrest

#### Page notes

*[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/22444de2a65680a1afbac18ce6183003)*

- Context
  - Served under George Washington as Vice President
  - First official Federalist President, though his principles and values aligned with Washington
- Rise of the Federalists and the Democratic-Republicans
  - Federalists: Generally wealthy and educated people, particularly from the eastern seaboard; supported the institution of a national bank, strong central government, and a loose interpretation of the Constitution
    - Also supported fixing the relationship with Great britain for trade purposes
  - Democratic Republicans: Typically westerners and farmers. Favored stronger state governments. Believed in a strict interpretation of the Constitution
    - Supported relationship with France
- The XYZ Affair
  - Jay’s Treaty: 1794: George Washington sends John Jay to the Supreme Court to negotiate a treaty with Great Britain to remove British forts from the northwest territory; in return would settle debts with British merchants
  - Jay’s Treaty angered the French, who had supported the US during the revolution; began attacking American Merchant ships
  - 1797: John Adams sends diplomats to forge a treaty with France
    - 3 diplomats, X, Y, and Z proceeded to ask for bribes to start negotiations; American public found out and wrote letters to Adams pushing for armed conflict with the French
  - US carried on an undeclared naval war with France
- The Alien and Sedition Acts
  - Fear of opposition to war led Federalists to look for a way to punish opposers (especially Anti-Federalists)
  - Alien Act was created to allow the government to deport non-citizens who were a threat to national security
  - Sedition: Writing or speaking in a way that promotes rebellion against the government
    - Sedition Act was *actually* meant to just punish Americans criticizing the government’s involvement in the war w/ intent to harm the government’s position
  - Under Sedition Act, US government punished several printers speaking out against the US; Matthew Lyon jailed for criticizing President John Adams
- The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions
  - Democratic-Republicans didn’t support the Alien and Sedition Acts, believing it gave the federal government too much power
  - Thomas Jefferson and James Madison each wrote resolutions that were adopted by Kentucky (Jefferson) and Virginia (Madison) that pushed for strict interpretations of the Constitution, claiming that states had the power to ignore federal laws when they considered them unconstitutional
  - These debates continued in the Nullification Crisis leading up to the civil war
- Adams’s Midnight Appointments
  - At the end of Adams’s presidency, he tried appointing as many Federalists as possible to positions of power - midnight appointments to “stack” the courts against Democratic-Republicans
    - Adams’s judicial appointments weren’t delivered in time. One of the judges who didn’t get his spot as a result, William Marbury, sued for the appointment, resulting in Marbury vs Madison, which established the principle of judicial review (Supreme Court can strike down laws if it judges the laws violate the Constitution)
