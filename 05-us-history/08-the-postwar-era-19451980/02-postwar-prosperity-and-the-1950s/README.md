### Growth of Suburbia (1940–1970)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24944de2a65680c48b56ed9c68e54f58)*

Americans moved from cities to suburbs w/ GI bill benefits that helped with home loans, and mass production expedited home building.

Growth of the American Dream

**Sources**

- [Khan outline on the growth of suburbia](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/postwar-era/a/the-growth-of-suburbia)
- [Khan outline on dark side of suburbia](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/postwar-era/a/the-dark-side-of-suburbia)

<!-- page-notes:24944de2a65680c48b56ed9c68e54f58 -->
#### Page notes

- Context
  - Postwar era
    - Many Americans that previously couldn’t could now afford to own homes in the suburbs
      - Reorganization of money and power
    - Factories previously dedicated to airplanes and barracks needed to fill new needs (lots more soldiers with no housing available
- New Suburbs
  - Levitt and Sons mass-produced homes in 3 suburbs:
    - Levittown: 4000 acres of potato fields in Long Island converted to homes
    - Largest private housing project in American History
    - Assembly line process with 27 distinct steps, and precision
      - Trees planted every 28 feet, each house had exactly the same floorplan, only \$7000 (or \$29 per month for a mortgage) vs \$90/month for apartment in the city
    - FHA and VA: homeowners now could use unprecedented credit for down payment, when previously yaverage was 58% for down payment
    - Built 2 more highly-successful suburbs in Pennsylvania and New Jersey (both named Levittown) and other developers adopted their own formulas for suburban housing
  - Resulted in push for cars: residents needed to own cars to go to work or purchase groceries; also expanded road network
  - Defense plants drew workers during the war, so more Americans moved to the warmer states of the Sunbelt for jobs, particularly in California and Florida
- Criticisms
  - Focus on sameness and conformity
  - Women now pushed more into the role of a housewife and trapped in suburbia
    - Note that 30% of women worked outside the home in some capacity during the 1950s
    - Advertising and popular culture counseled women that greatest satisfaction comes from raising children, tending to their husbands’ needs, and owning all the labor-saving household appliances they could buy
    - Sense there ought to be more to life than childcare and housework
  - Suburbia was segregationist
    - Restrictive covenants barred most non-white families from living in suburban neighborhoods
    - Although Supreme Court in 1948 ruled that restrictive covenants were unenforceable, violence and intimidation still worked to keep them out
    - Banks refused to loan money for new homes or improvements in inner city neighborhoods (redlining)

### The GI Bill (1944–1946)

*Historical Events · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24644de2a656808cb7eff369d90b17db)*

Sent WWII veterans to school, backed home loans, gave unemployment benefits to veterans and gave veterans medical care

Fueled economic prosperity that characterized postwar era; boosted education

Demonstrated that the US government was obligated to protect the rights of Americans, including the right to work and education as compensation for military service. Created the Affluent American society of the 1950s and 1960s, but uneven distribution of its benefits would have ramifications, including resentment of African Americans and women.

**Sources**

- [Khan outline of the GI Bill](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/postwar-era/a/the-gi-bill)
- [Khan outline of African Americans, women, and the GI Bill](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/postwar-era/a/african-americans-women-and-the-gi-bill)

<summary>Overview</summary>

- Benefits
  - Sent 8+ million WWII veterans to school between 1945 and 1956
  - Backed home loans
  - Gave veterans a year of unemployment benefits
  - Provided for veterans’ medical care
- Leadup
  - US government anxious - 15 million veterans would reenter the workforce post-WWII; needed to provide them with financial security on their return and siphon them into educational programs
- Put into law by FDR by Servicemen’s Readjustment Act (1944)/GI Bill
  - Previously, FDR won broad support through the New Deal (used government programs to ensure baseline economic security for the American people
  - Roosevelt proposed “economic bill of rights” which would guarantee American citizens employment, healthcare, education, and housing
    - with wartime economic boom, this promise failed to animate voters as it had a decade earlier
- Pushback
  - Education previously reserved for the elite; “education is not a device for coping with mass unemployment … colleges and universities will find themselves converted into educational hobo jungles.” (-president of the University of Chicago)
- Huge success
  - Despite high pricetag (\$14.5 billion), generated ten times the cost of the program in tax revenue
  - Considered “the best investment the US government has ever made”
  - Expanded curriculum to a wide range of career paths, including science, business, and engineering
  - Prompted an economic boom, w/ construction of thousands of new homes and suburbs

</details>

<details>
<summary>African Americans, Women, and the GI Bill</summary>

- Technically entitled to the same benefits as white men, but in practice faced difficulty claiming their benefits b/c of discrimination
  - Federally funded, but steered at a state and local level by Veterans Administration (very white, closely affiliated with pro-segregation American Legion)
    - Steered African Americans to vocational training and military jobs; told the job market had no place for Blacks as skilled workers
  - Colleges had caps on the number of Black students they would admit
    - Most Black veterans elected to attend Historically Black Colleges and Universities (HBCUs) when possible, even when they had to delay due to overcrowding. They were few and far between in the north
  - Many banks refused to loan money to Blacks, and suburban neighborhoods often had restrictive covenants that banned African American families from purchasing homes there; confining them to inner cities
- Frustration of African American veterans barred from participating in the postwar economic boom prompted the Civil Rights Movement
- Women and the GI bill
  - Only 65,000 female veterans (of the 332,000) attended college through the GI bill, but a higher percentage of them opted for university education vs men (as opposed to vocational training)
    - Women’s branches of the armed services were more selective; limited the women in their ranks to the highly-trained and educated.
      - \~70% of women veterans were prepared for college
  - Often informed they were not eligible or faced hostility
  - Fewer women during this time period received college degrees overall because colleges limited female enrollment to make space for male veterans

</details>

### The Baby Boom (1946–1964)

*Historical Periods · American · [Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24944de2a65680a99c21ef900874e9a6)*

Spike in birth rate following WWII, strong postwar economy, and general confidence that a larger number of children could be supported.

**Sources**

- [Khan Outline](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/us-history/postwarera/postwar-era/a/the-baby-boom)

<!-- page-notes:24944de2a65680a99c21ef900874e9a6 -->
#### Page notes

*[Image in Notion: Norman Rockwell, *Freedom from Want*, 1943. This Office of War information poster illustrated one of the freedoms the Allies were fighting for during WWII. Images like these may have induced Americans to crave domesticity and family life in the postwar era.](https://app.notion.com/p/24944de2a65680a99c21ef900874e9a6)*

- Context
  - Prior to the baby boom, American birthrate fell to its lowest point yet - 18.4 live births per thousand population
    - Americans moved off the farm and into the city; large family transformed from labor investment to a poor economic choice
  - Post-War, soldiers returned home and wished to settle down into family life
    - GI bill benefits promised the decent pay, access to good jobs, and affordable housing
    - Couples rushed to wed and conceive children before soldiers shipped out — romance and urgency of wartime, extra pay to soldiers with families
  - Women and men both had reliable forms of birth control - conscious decision to have more children
  - Devotion to domesticity following the war
    - Government propaganda extolled virtues of “aapple-pie American life”, normal life
    - Sanctuary from fears wrt atomic bomb
- Resulted in a large generation raised in prosperity
  - Increased advertising, production, etc - toys, candy, washing machines, etc
  - Drove creation of new suburbs and schools
  - “Echo Boom” generation of children 1976-2001
- Forefront of social change in the 1960s and 1970s
  - Civil Rights Movement later stages
  - Protest against the Vietnam War
  - Second wave of the feminist movement
- Ratio of baby boomers to the rest of the population will place strain on Social Security, hospitals, and other government agencies to help the elderly
  - Census Bureau estimates 1 in 5 Americans will be over the age of 65 in 2030
  - US birth rate currently at an all-time low (12.5 live births per thousand population)
  - By 2056, Americans aged 65 or older will outnumber those under the age of 18

<details>
<summary>Anticommunism and the Red Scare</summary>

*[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24b44de2a65680feb6b8e83a73b7bb69)*

- Nationwide panic that spies in the US atomic program passed secrets to the Soviet Union, and spies could be in other institutions as well
  - Those suspected to be susceptible to bribery (like debtors and homosexuals) were easily dismissed and barred from jobs and trust
- Allegations that Hollywood was rife with communists: House Committee on Un-American Activities (HUAC) investigated actors, writers, and directors during the 1950s; blacklist from working in Hollywood
- Republican Senator Joseph McCarthy accused State Department employees of communist leanings to advance himself politically
  - “McCarthyism”

</details>

<details>
<summary>Popular Culture and Mass Media</summary>

- Young Americans had more disposable income and enjoyed greater material comfort; devoted more time and money to leisure activities
- Rock and Roll drew inspiration from African American Blues music; themes included young love and rebellion against authority
  - Popular musicians: Elvis Presley, Chuck Berry, Little Richard, Bill Haley and His Comets, Buddy Holly, Ray Charles
    - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24b44de2a65680feb6b8e83a73b7bb69)*
- Introduction of the television
  - *[Image in Notion](https://app.notion.com/p/24b44de2a65680feb6b8e83a73b7bb69)*
  - Popular television shows: The Ed Sullivan Show, I Love Lucy, Leave it to Beaver, The Honeymooners, The Lone Ranger, The Roy Rogers Show, Lassie, The Mickey Mouse Club
  - Trouble for movies and Hollywood
    - Competition with television
    - Legal concerns with vertical integration (studios would have their own theater chains, which violated anti trust laws - United States vs Paramount Pictures).
    - House Un-American Activities Committee: the Red Scare caused blacklisting in Hollywood for anyone suspected of communist leanings; unverified accounts, unfriendly witnesses, and accusing others for political gain

</details>

<details>
<summary>Women in the 1950s</summary>

- Men and Women observed strict gender roles, complying with society’s expectations
  - Following the Great Depression and WWII, Americans sought comfort and peace.
- Changes in patterns of sexual behavior (led to the “sexual revolution” of the 1960s)
  - Rise in premarital sex and birth rates
  - Cars facilitated rise of new sexual norms (freedom, ability to find space together alone)
- Women encouraged by popular culture and mass media to abandon their jobs and return to domestic life; but most women kept their jobs, making up approximately 1/3 of the labor force.
  - 40% of women with young children, and at least half of women with older children, rmained in the work force
  - Pushed the link between traditional gender roles and national security
- African American Women not portrayed in popular culture at all; often needed to work due to economic necessity, were barred from suburbia, and otherwise excluded from the picture of ideal domesticity

</details>

<details>
<summary>The Arms Race</summary>

- US first tested hydrogen bomb in 1952 - Americans feared nuclear war could happen at any time
  - Scientists who developed it formed the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists - dedicated to alerting the world to the dangers of nuclear weaponry
    - J Robert Oppenheimer, the director of the Manhattan Project
    - Einstein, who dedicated the final years of his life to promoting nuclear disarmament.
    - First magazine included the Doomsday Clock to show how close humanity was to nuclear annhilation
  - Fears that the Soviet Union developed the hydrogen bomb meant that the US reasoned they needed to develop it as well; became an arms race despite the worry of a nuclear holocaust
- Government urged citizens to prepare to survive an atomic bomb
  - Dogtags issued to children at school so that families could identify their bodies in the event of an attack
  - Nuclear bomb drills became routine
  - Civil Defense film [Duck and Cover](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IKqXu-5jw60) shown to children to help them protect themselves in a nuclear attack; wouldn’t have done anything to help, but gave Americans a sense of control
- US created the National Security Council, which issued reports on world affairs and how to confront the crisis
  - NSC-68: Cast the tension as an apocalyptic battle between good and evil. Recommended that the US drastically increase its national security spending, and kicked off with the Korean War
    - Assumed that the Soviet Union had both the capacity and the desire to take over the world, neither of which was necessarily true
- Massive Retaliation: Maximum defense capabilities at an affordable cost
  - If the US is attacked, it can rely on “mutually-assured destruction”
  - US feared to protect Hungary when attacked by Soviets in 1956 for fear it would antagonize the Soviet Union and trigger a nuclear war
- Eisenhower warned of the dangers posed by the growing influence of the military-industrial complex but could not slow the arms race

</details>

<details>
<summary>The Space Race</summary>

- Early Soviet successes in the space race affected US society and culture: new educational initiatives and altered defense doctrines
  - Soviets launched Sputnik I in October 1957: world’s first artificial satellite
    - Orbited the earth and transmitted radio signals for 21 days before burning up in the atmosphere
    - Sputnik II, launched a month later, carried a dog named Laika
    - In 1959, Soviets launched Lunik III to photograph the dark side of the moon
  - NASA created in 1958 in response response to Soviet successes
  - Soviets were first to put a manned vehicle into Earth’s orbit: Yuri Gagarin via Vostok I in 1961
    - Alan Shepard became first American in space a month later in May 1961, and in 1962, John Glenn became first American to enter Earth’s orbit
  - JFK announced inauguration of the Apollo program to put a man on the moon by the end of the decade
- National Defense Education Act
  - Envisioned public education as a key component of national security
    - \$800 million for loans to college students and for states to improve instruction in the hard sciences
    - Covered student loans, foreign languages (including Russian and Mandarin Chinese) and bolstered public education
  - Fear that the Soviet advancements could be used to launch intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs) to deliver nuclear warheads to targets in the US - new drive to close the “missile gap”
    - Later released documents indicate that the missile gap never existed, and US maintained clear superiority in missile technology

</details>
